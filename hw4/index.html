Overview

In this homework we built a real-time cloth simulator from scratch. We started by modeling the cloth as a grid of point masses connected by structural, shearing, and bending springs, then implemented Verlet integration to step the simulation forward in time — applying gravity, spring correction forces, and a Provot deformation constraint to keep the cloth physically plausible. From there we added collision handling with external objects (spheres and planes) and self-collision detection using spatial hashing, which lets the cloth fold realistically on itself without clipping.

The second half of the project focused on GPU shading. We implemented a full Blinn-Phong shader, texture mapping, bump mapping, displacement mapping, and an environment-mapped mirror shader. For extra credit we wrote a custom animated shader that combines cel shading, scrolling scanlines, a rainbow Fresnel rim, vertex-level glitch displacement, and a neon bloom pass to produce a holographic effect.

The most interesting thing we learned was how much the order of operations matters in a physics simulation — small changes to when constraints and collisions are applied can make the difference between stable, realistic behavior and a cloth that explodes or passes through geometry. We also found spatial hashing to be an elegant solution to what would otherwise be an O(n²) self-collision problem, and it was satisfying to see how GPU parallelism makes complex per-pixel shading computationally viable in real time.

Effect of Spring Constant (ks)

When adjusting ks from 30 to 6000, lower ks values produce a cloth that feels softer and more easily deformable. The fabric stretches more, sags under gravity, and doesn't hold its shape well — the springs are weak and don't strongly pull the cloth back to its original configuration. At ks = 30, the top edge of the cloth curves dramatically downward between the two pinned corners, forming a wide arch, and the fabric gathers in loose folds toward the bottom.

As ks increases, the cloth becomes more stiff and structured since the springs resist deformation more aggressively. At ks = 6000, the top edge is nearly flat and the cloth hangs almost rigidly from the pins with far less drape. However, the behavior at this value is also less stable: the strong spring forces cause the system to overshoot its rest positions, leading to noticeable oscillations and rippling during motion. So while higher ks corresponds to higher stiffness in theory, in practice extremely high values introduce oscillations that make the cloth look dynamic and wavy rather than rigid.

Effect of Density

Lower density values produce a much lighter cloth. It falls more slowly and carries less momentum, so it settles quickly and doesn't swing or overshoot as much. At 2 g/cm², the cloth hangs nearly straight down from the two pins with very little sag — the fabric barely responds to gravity.

As density increases, the cloth becomes heavier and falls more forcefully under gravity. At 30 g/cm², the cloth builds significantly more momentum during the fall, producing a deep, asymmetric drape as the heavy fabric pulls strongly downward. The bottom portion shifts and gathers under its own weight before settling.

Effect of Damping

Lower damping values allow the cloth to retain more of its kinetic energy over time. The cloth continues to oscillate and ripple long after the initial drop — folds and waves that develop during the fall persist instead of dying out.

Higher damping causes motion to die out much faster. The cloth settles into a clean, flat resting shape quickly — oscillations are suppressed early, so the final state appears smooth and stable without visible wrinkles from the motion. Part 3: Handling collisions with other objects

Implementation

Sphere collisions: For each point mass, I check whether it is inside the sphere by computing the distance from the point mass's position to the sphere's origin. If this distance is less than or equal to the sphere's radius, a collision has occurred. To resolve it, I compute the tangent point by extending the ray from the sphere's origin through the point mass's current position out to the sphere's surface — done by normalizing the direction vector and scaling it by the radius. I then compute a correction vector from last_position to the tangent point, and set the new position to last_position plus the correction vector scaled by (1 - friction).

Plane collisions: For each point mass, I check whether it has crossed the plane between its last_position and current position by computing the dot product of each with the plane's normal. If the signs differ (or one is zero), the point mass has crossed over. I compute the tangent point using the parametric intersection of the point mass's path with the plane, then offset it slightly by SURFACE_OFFSET in the direction of the side the point mass came from. The correction vector goes from last_position to this offset tangent point, and the new position is last_position plus the correction scaled by (1 - friction).

Part 4: Handling self-collisions

Implementation

To handle self-collisions efficiently, we implemented spatial hashing — a technique that partitions 3D space into fixed-size boxes and groups point masses by which box they occupy, reducing the collision check from O(n²) to roughly O(n).

hash_position: Each point mass position is mapped to a unique float key by first computing box dimensions w = 3 * width / num_width_points, h = 3 * height / num_height_points, and t = max(w, h). The position is then truncated to the nearest box by flooring each coordinate divided by the box dimension. A unique hash is computed from these truncated coordinates using large prime multipliers to minimize collisions.

build_spatial_map: At the start of each simulation step, the spatial map is cleared and rebuilt by iterating over all point masses, hashing their positions with hash_position, and inserting each into the corresponding bucket in the map.

self_collide: For each point mass, we look up all candidates sharing the same spatial bucket. For each candidate (excluding the point mass itself), we check if the two are within 2 * thickness of each other. If so, we compute a correction vector that would push them exactly 2 * thickness apart along the vector between them. The final correction applied to the point mass is the average of all such pairwise corrections, scaled down by simulation_steps to avoid sudden large position jumps.

Part 5: Shaders

What is a Shader Program?

A shader program is a small program that runs directly on the GPU in parallel for every vertex or every pixel being rendered. Instead of the CPU calculating lighting and color for each point one at a time, the GPU runs thousands of these shader programs simultaneously, making real-time rendering possible.

Vertex and fragment shaders work together in a pipeline. The vertex shader runs first, processing each vertex of the mesh — transforming its position from model space into screen space and computing per-vertex properties like world-space position and normal, which it passes along as "varying" outputs. The fragment shader runs second: for every pixel a triangle covers on screen, it receives interpolated versions of those varying outputs and uses them to compute the final pixel color. So the vertex shader handles geometry and the fragment shader handles appearance and lighting.

Bump Mapping vs. Displacement Mapping

Bump mapping modifies the surface normals at each fragment to simulate the illusion of geometric detail without actually changing the mesh geometry. The silhouette of the object remains perfectly smooth and only the lighting reacts as if there are bumps. This is computationally cheaper and works well at any mesh resolution since it operates entirely in the fragment shader.

Displacement mapping actually moves each vertex along its normal direction based on the height map value, physically deforming the mesh geometry. This means the silhouette of the object is genuinely bumpy and the geometry change is real. However, this requires a sufficiently dense mesh to look good; with too few vertices, the displacement looks blocky and coarse.

Effect of Mesh Coarseness

-o 16 -a 16 (coarse mesh): Bump mapping looks essentially the same as on a fine mesh since it only affects normals in the fragment shader — surface detail appears normal. Displacement mapping looks very blocky and jagged: with only 16×16 vertices there aren't enough points to represent the smooth displacement, so the sphere looks like a low-poly deformed shape rather than a naturally bumpy surface.

-o 128 -a 128 (fine mesh): Bump mapping looks the same, confirming that mesh resolution doesn't affect bump mapping. Displacement mapping looks much more natural and detailed — the higher vertex count allows the displacement to follow the height map closely, producing a convincingly rough surface whose silhouette matches the texture detail.

Mirror Shader

The mirror shader uses environment mapping to reflect the surrounding cubemap on the cloth and sphere surfaces, creating a realistic reflective material.

(Optional) Part 6: Extra Credit - Custom Shader: Neon Rainbow Hologram

The custom shader combines five effects to create an animated holographic appearance.

Toon Diffuse: Instead of smooth Phong shading, the diffuse lighting value is quantized into 4 discrete bands using floor(), giving the surface a flat cel-shaded look. The base color is kept near-black so the neon effects read clearly against it.

Scrolling Scanlines: Two sine waves at different frequencies (coarse and fine) are computed in world-space Y, offset by u_time so they scroll upward continuously. These are multiplied into the diffuse to simulate the horizontal line pattern of a CRT or holographic display.

Rainbow Fresnel Rim: A Fresnel term (1 - dot(N, V) raised to a power) detects silhouette edges. That value is multiplied by a color computed from HSV, where the hue is driven by u_time and world-space Y position, so the rim continuously cycles through the full rainbow and varies by height on the mesh.

Glitch Displacement: Two independent glitch systems run simultaneously. In the vertex shader, world-space Y is quantized into horizontal slabs and a hash function occasionally snaps entire slabs sideways in X for a frame. In the fragment shader, a separate hash independently shifts the UV coordinates for the red and blue channels in opposite directions, creating chromatic aberration on random rows at ~4Hz.

Neon Bloom: After compositing all layers, a luminance value is computed and bright pixels receive an additional additive pass of the current rim color using smoothstep, simulating HDR glow without a full post-processing pipeline.
